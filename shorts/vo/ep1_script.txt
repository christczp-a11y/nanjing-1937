In November 1937, a Chinese general promised to live or die with his capital. Less than four weeks later the capital was gone, and he was on the other side of the river. This is how Nanjing became a trap.
It starts two hundred miles away, in Shanghai. For three months China throws its best divisions into that city. In November the line breaks.
The Japanese army was sent to take Shanghai. Its commanders do not stop there. A hundred and sixty thousand men chase the retreat west, up the Yangtze, toward the capital.
Nanjing has been China's capital for ten years. That spring the city counted one million and nineteen thousand people. By December most of those who could leave have left. Somewhere between a quarter and half a million are still inside.
Around them is a wall thirty-five kilometres long. Behind it is the river. A kilometre of water, and no bridge.
Most of China's generals want to give the city up and save the army. Chiang Kai-shek refuses. He moves the government inland, and gives the city to the one general who says it can be held.
That general has between seventy and a hundred and fifty thousand men. Nobody agrees on the number. Half of them are recruits. So he takes away their way out. He walls up the gates. He sends the ferries upriver.
On December the eighth, the last line outside the city breaks. By the next morning seventy thousand Japanese soldiers are at the wall, on three sides.
On the fourth side is the river.
