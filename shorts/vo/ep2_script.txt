On December the ninth, 1937, Japanese planes dropped a letter on the Chinese capital. Surrender by noon tomorrow. Nobody answered.
At one o'clock, seventy thousand men attacked a wall built in the fourteenth century.
In the south-east, engineers blew a hole in a gate. Two companies ran in and could not get out. One of them lost eighty of its eighty-eight men.
In the east, the attackers were ordered not to shell one side of the mountain, because the tomb of modern China's founder was on it. They took the mountain ridge by ridge instead. Then they turned their guns on the wall below.
In the south, forty thousand men needed two days to take one hill. Almost nobody on it came down.
On the twelfth, the wall broke.
At five that afternoon the general ordered his army out. Break through the enemy, the order said. Twelve and a half thousand men tried it, in the dark. Three or four thousand got through.
Everyone else ran for the river gate. It was half blocked, and its guards had not been told. They opened fire.
The general crossed that night in a steamboat. There were no boats for anyone else. One division reached the water with three thousand men. Three hundred lived.
Japan counted its own dead for the whole battle. One thousand five hundred and fifty-eight.
For the Chinese army there is no agreed number at all.
At dawn, the Japanese walked in.
