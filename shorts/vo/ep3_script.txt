On December the thirteenth, 1937, the battle for Nanjing was over. For the people still inside, the worst six weeks were about to begin.
The day before, twenty-eight miles upriver, Japanese planes had sunk an American gunboat. That made the front pages. What was happening in the city took three more days to get out.
Soldiers who could not escape threw away their uniforms. The army went looking for them. It checked men's hands. Calluses meant a rifle. The men it picked were tied together, walked to the river, and shot.
At one place on the bank the army counted its prisoners. Fourteen thousand, seven hundred and seventy-seven. They were machine-gunned over three nights.
In the west of the city, about twenty foreigners had drawn a line and called it a safety zone. A quarter of a million people got inside it. The man in charge was a member of the Nazi Party. It was safer. It was not safe. A court later counted twenty thousand rapes in the first month.
There were seventy thousand soldiers in the city. The army gave them seventeen military policemen.
On the seventeenth the Japanese commander rode in for his victory parade.
On the nineteenth his army began to burn the city. A third of it.
The people who buried the dead counted a hundred and fifty-five thousand bodies. A court in Tokyo found that more than two hundred thousand people were murdered. A court in Nanjing found more than three hundred thousand.
The commander was hanged in 1948. The wall is still there.
